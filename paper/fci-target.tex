\begin{abstract}
\noindent Financial conditions indices are routinely evaluated against a
recursively estimated autoregression. For a near-unit-root target in a short
sample that benchmark is biased toward the sample mean, and the bias compounds
with the forecast horizon. We show analytically and by simulation that a random
walk should be the better benchmark once $1-\rho$ falls below $(1+3\rho)/T$,
and we confirm the prediction on eleven euro-area regions: the random walk
forecasts the unemployment rate more accurately than the autoregression in ten
of them at every horizon, by a median of 33\% of root mean squared error at two
years. The consequences for measurement are large. Against the better of a
random walk and a recursive mean, a financial conditions index improves
unemployment forecasts one and two quarters ahead --- in ten and nine of the
eleven regions, with panel Clark--West statistics of 4.0 and 3.2 --- but at two
years it loses in every region and the panel statistic is $-1.0$. Against the
estimated autoregression alone, the same forecasts appear to win at every
horizon, with a panel statistic of 2.3 at two years. The test is not at fault:
in simulation its rejection rate against the autoregression is insensitive to
the target's persistence, while the forecaster's verdict reverses. A test of
whether a variable enters a regression is not a test of whether that regression
is worth using.

\vspace{1em}
\noindent \textbf{Keywords:} financial conditions index; forecast evaluation;
benchmark selection; near-unit-root bias; euro area

\noindent \textbf{JEL:} C53, C22, E44, E37
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Financial conditions indices compress many financial series into one number,
and the case for them rests on whether that number helps forecast real
activity. The standard evaluation regresses a target variable at $t+h$ on its
own lag and the index, and compares the resulting forecast errors with those of
the same regression estimated without it
\citep{koop2014, hatzius2010, stock2002}.

The own-lag regression is the natural benchmark and, for this class of target,
a poor one. An unemployment rate is close to a unit root. Least squares
estimation of an autoregressive coefficient is biased downward in finite
samples \citep{marriott1954, kendall1954}, and the resulting forecast is pulled
toward a sample mean from which the level has drifted away. Iterating that
attenuated coefficient forward makes the distortion compound with the horizon.
A random walk, which imposes the unit root rather than estimating it, is wrong
by $1-\rho^h$ and does not pay the estimation cost at all.

This paper asks what that choice is worth, and answers in three steps.

First, we characterise when the random walk should be preferred. Combining the
standard small-sample bias with the geometry of an iterated forecast gives a
criterion: the estimated autoregression is the better benchmark only when
$1-\rho$ exceeds $(1+3\rho)/T$, which at $T = 80$ means $\rho$ below about
$0.95$. A Monte Carlo locates the crossover between $\rho = 0.90$ and
$\rho = 0.95$, where the criterion puts it.

Second, we show the criterion binds in practice. Across the euro area and ten
member countries, the random walk forecasts the unemployment rate more
accurately than a recursively estimated own-lag regression in ten of the eleven
regions at every horizon, and its margin widens from a median of 4\% of root
mean squared error at one quarter to 33\% at two years. The single exception,
Austria, is the one region whose unemployment rate mean-reverts over this
sample rather than drifting --- that is, the one region below the crossover.

Third, we show what this does to the measured value of an index. Against the
better of the two competent benchmarks, the index of \citet{koop2014} improves
unemployment forecasts at one and two quarters --- in ten and nine of eleven
regions, with panel Clark--West statistics of $4.0$ and $3.2$ --- and loses in
all eleven regions at two years, where the panel statistic is $-1.0$. Against
the estimated autoregression alone, the same forecasts appear to win at one,
two, four and eight quarters in eleven, eleven, eleven and ten regions, with a
panel statistic of $2.3$ at two years.

We emphasise what this is not. It is not a finding that the Clark--West test
malfunctions. The test asks whether the index belongs in the population
regression that also contains an estimated own lag, and the answer is yes. Our
simulations show its rejection rate against that benchmark to be essentially
flat in the target's persistence, while the forecaster's verdict reverses over
the same range. Both facts are correct simultaneously, because they answer
different questions. The difficulty is that a model containing a badly
estimated autoregressive coefficient is not a useful yardstick for a forecaster
who could impose a unit root at no cost.

Our positive finding is narrower than the literature's and, we would argue,
more secure. Financial conditions carry genuine information about unemployment
one and two quarters ahead, across eleven countries, against a benchmark that
is hard to beat, and under inference that accounts for the cross-sectional
dependence among them. Evidence of predictive content at a year or more, when
it rests on an autoregressive benchmark, may be measuring the benchmark's
deterioration rather than the index's information.

\section{Evaluation design}

\subsection{The index}

Let $y_t$ collect output growth, inflation and the unemployment rate, and $x_t$
the financial series. The index is the first factor $f_t$ of the
time-varying-parameter FAVAR of \citet{koop2014},
\begin{align}
x_t &= \Lambda^y_t y_t + \Lambda^f_t f_t + e_t, \qquad e_t \sim N(0, V_t), \\
z_t &= \sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{p} B_{it} z_{t-i} + u_t, \qquad u_t \sim N(0, Q_t),
\end{align}
with $z_t = (y_t', f_t')'$. Loadings and transition coefficients follow random
walks; the two error covariances and the drift are governed by forgetting
factors rather than by estimated variances, so the model follows from two
Kalman passes rather than from posterior simulation. We use the authors'
settings throughout ($p = 4$, one factor, forgetting factors $0.96$, $0.96$,
$0.99$, $0.99$) and fix the sign of the factor on the economic sentiment
indicator. The macroeconomic block enters the measurement equation, so the
factor is by construction what the financial series say beyond $y_t$.

Nothing below depends on this particular index. We use it because it is a
standard construction with a public implementation, and because holding the
index fixed is what isolates the benchmark.

\subsection{Recursive forecasts and benchmarks}

The evaluation is recursive. At each origin $t$ the factor model is
re-estimated on data through $t$ only, yielding the index path a user would
have had at that date. Four forecasts of the target $w_{t+h}$ are then formed,
each using data through $t$ only:
\begin{align}
\text{recursive mean:} \quad & \widehat{w} = \textstyle\frac{1}{t-h}\sum_{s \le t-h} w_{s},
& \text{random walk:} \quad & \widehat{w} = u_t, \\
\text{own lag:} \quad & \widehat{w} = \hat\alpha + \hat\beta u_t,
& \text{index:} \quad & \widehat{w} = \hat\alpha + \hat\beta u_t + \hat\gamma f_t .
\label{eq:pred}
\end{align}
The regression coefficients are re-estimated at every origin on the same
restricted information set, so nothing used at $t$ postdates $t$. All three
benchmarks are nested in the index regression: the recursive mean sets
$\beta = \gamma = 0$, the random walk fixes $\alpha = 0$, $\beta = 1$,
$\gamma = 0$. We report the index against whichever benchmark achieved the
lowest root mean squared error in each cell, and separately against the own-lag
regression alone.

We take the target to be the average of the variable over $t+1, \dots, t+h$
rather than its value in the single quarter $t+h$. Financial conditions tighten
and loosen over stretches of quarters and unemployment responds over stretches
of quarters, so the path is the quantity an index plausibly speaks to; the two
coincide at $h = 1$. Section~\ref{sec:robust} reports the single-quarter
target, which is less favourable to the index at every horizon beyond the
first, so this choice makes the negative result conservative rather than the
reverse.

\subsection{Inference}

Because the models are nested, an unadjusted \citet{diebold1995} test is
undersized: under the null the larger model estimates a coefficient that is
truly zero, and the noise in doing so inflates its squared error.
\citet{clark2007} remove that penalty, comparing
\begin{equation}
\hat d_t = \big(w_{t+h} - \widehat{w}^{\,b}_{t+h}\big)^2
 - \Big[\big(w_{t+h} - \widehat{w}^{\,f}_{t+h}\big)^2
 - \big(\widehat{w}^{\,b}_{t+h} - \widehat{w}^{\,f}_{t+h}\big)^2\Big]
\end{equation}
against zero, where $b$ denotes the benchmark and $f$ the index regression.
Overlapping $h$-step forecasts are handled with a Bartlett kernel of $h-1$
lags, and we use the one-sided normal critical value of $1.645$.

Eleven euro-area regions are not eleven independent experiments. They share a
monetary policy and much of a business cycle, and the average pairwise
correlation of the $\hat d_t$ across regions runs from $0.35$ at one quarter to
$0.04$ at two years. Counting individual rejections would overstate the
evidence. We instead average the adjusted differentials across regions at each
origin and test the average,
\begin{equation}
\bar d_t = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \hat d_{i,t}, \qquad
t = \frac{\bar d}{\sqrt{\widehat{\mathrm{lrv}}(\bar d_t)/T_o}},
\end{equation}
with $T_o$ the number of common origins. Averaging first absorbs the common
component into the time-series variance of $\bar d_t$, so the statistic does
not treat correlated countries as independent. We report both the per-region
counts and this panel statistic.

\section{Data}

We use EA-MD-QD \citep{barigozzi2024, eamdqd}, which provides quarterly series
for the euro area aggregate and ten member countries on a common definition
from 2000\,Q1: Austria, Belgium, Germany, Greece, Spain, France, Ireland, Italy, the
Netherlands and Portugal. The financial block has twenty-two series per region
--- equity and house prices, effective and dollar exchange rates, short and
long interest rates and three spreads, sectoral balance sheets for households,
firms and banks, four confidence balances and two global risk measures ---
with four further national series for Austria and Germany. The macroeconomic
block is output growth, inflation and the unemployment rate.

Forecast origins run from 2006\,Q1 to the end of the sample, giving 72 to 79
forecasts per region and horizon. The dataset carries no real-time vintages; we
return to this in Section~\ref{sec:disc}.

\section{Why an estimated autoregression fails}
\label{sec:why}

\subsection{The bias and its horizon gradient}

Write the target as $w_t = \mu + \rho(w_{t-1}-\mu) + \varepsilon_t$. Least
squares estimation of $\rho$ on $T$ observations with an estimated intercept is
biased downward, with \citet{marriott1954} and \citet{kendall1954} giving
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}[\hat\rho] - \rho \;\approx\; -\,\frac{1+3\rho}{T} .
\label{eq:bias}
\end{equation}
The bias depends only weakly on $\rho$ and is of order $1/T$: at $T = 80$ it is
close to $0.05$ throughout the range of interest. Simulation confirms the
approximation --- at $T = 80$ the mean estimate lies within $0.015$ of
\eqref{eq:bias} for every $\rho$ between $0.90$ and $1$.

Two consequences follow. First, the distortion compounds with the horizon. An
iterated $h$-step forecast scales the deviation $w_t - \hat\mu$ by
$\hat\rho^{\,h}$ rather than $\rho^h$, and for a bias $b$,
\begin{equation}
\rho^h - (\rho-b)^h \;\approx\; h\,\rho^{h-1} b ,
\end{equation}
so the attenuation grows roughly linearly in $h$ before geometric decay takes
over. At $\rho = 0.99$ and $T = 80$ the implied attenuation of an eight-step
forecast is about 40\% of its correct magnitude.

Second, the random walk's error runs the other way and does not depend on $T$.
Imposing a unit root scales the deviation by $1$ rather than $\rho^h$, an error
of $1-\rho^h$, and costs nothing to estimate. Comparing the two at $h = 1$, the
estimated autoregression is the better benchmark only when
\begin{equation}
1-\rho \;>\; \frac{1+3\rho}{T} ,
\label{eq:crossover}
\end{equation}
which at $T = 80$ places the crossover at $\rho \approx 0.95$. Below it the
estimation bias is the smaller of the two errors and the autoregression should
win; above it the random walk should. At longer horizons the comparison turns
on $h\rho^{h-1}b$ against $1-\rho^h$, which for persistent targets tilts
further toward the random walk.

\subsection{Monte Carlo evidence}

Expression \eqref{eq:crossover} concerns a benchmark in isolation. What matters
for measurement is what it does to a predictor evaluated against that
benchmark, and for that we need a design in which the predictor's true value is
known. We simulate
\begin{equation}
x_t = \phi x_{t-1} + \eta_t, \qquad
w_t = \rho w_{t-1} + \theta x_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t,
\end{equation}
with $\eta_t, \varepsilon_t$ standard normal and independent, $\theta = 0.3$
and $T = 80$. Because $x_{t-1}$ enters the target directly, the predictor has
genuine predictive content at every horizon and every $\rho$: anything the
benchmark does wrong below is the benchmark's doing and not a missing signal.
We set $\phi = 0.4$ so that the first-order autocorrelation of $w_t$ stays
within $0.02$ of $\rho$; with a more persistent predictor, $x$ feeds its own
autocorrelation into $w$ and $\rho$ ceases to describe the target's
persistence. The four forecasts are formed exactly as in Section~2. Accuracy
results use 6{,}000 replications; the rejection frequencies in
Table~\ref{tab:siminf} use 500 replications of a full sequence of 79 origins,
so that each Clark--West statistic is computed as it is in the empirical
exercise.

The four-quarter cell deserves a word, because the ratio and the statistic
point different ways: the mean ratio is $1.052$ while the panel statistic is
$2.31$. They answer different questions, as \citet{clark2007} intend. The
statistic asks whether the index belongs in the population regression; the
ratio reports what a forecaster obtained after paying the cost of estimating
its coefficient. At a year the index's information is real but too small to
cover that cost in a sample of eighty quarters. We read the year horizon as
genuinely ambiguous and two years as clearly negative on both criteria.

\section{Robustness}
\label{sec:robust}

\textbf{Target definition.} Scoring the single quarter $t+h$ rather than the
path makes the index look worse at every horizon beyond the first: at four
quarters it wins in three regions rather than six, with a mean ratio of $1.106$
against $1.052$; at two years, $1.461$ against $1.316$. The path target is the
conservative choice for the negative result.

\textbf{Other target variables.} For output growth the recursive mean is the
best benchmark in all eleven regions and the index beats it in none at any
horizon, with mean ratios between $1.20$ and $1.65$. For inflation the index
wins in six or seven regions with mean ratios close to one and Clark--West
statistics clearing in at most five. Neither variable shows the short-horizon
result that unemployment does.

\textbf{Number of factors.} Extracting a second factor while continuing to use
only the first as a predictor leaves the counts in Table~\ref{tab:main}
unchanged and moves the mean ratios by less than a hundredth.

\textbf{Wider autoregressions.} Replacing the AR(1) benchmark with an AR(2) or
AR(4) makes the benchmark worse, not better --- the same disease rather than a
cure, since it estimates more coefficients on a near-unit-root series in a
short sample. Imposing the unit root is what helps.

\textbf{A longer sample.} Austria is the one region for which national sources
allow the panel to be extended, to 1993, giving 100 to 107 origins. The
short-horizon result survives, and so does Austria's atypicality: there the
own-lag regression is the best of the three benchmarks at every horizon,
including two years, where the index beats it narrowly.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:disc}

Three qualifications bear on how these numbers should be read.

The benchmark set is three simple rules. A properly specified univariate model
of each country's unemployment rate --- one that selects its own order, tests
for a unit root explicitly, or shrinks toward one --- would be harder to beat
than any of them, and our short-horizon result should be read as a comparison
against a random walk rather than against the state of the art. We would expect
the qualitative conclusion to survive, since the failure we document is
specific to estimating a coefficient that ought to be imposed, but we have not
shown it.

The data are not real time. EA-MD-QD contains no vintages, so the macroeconomic
variables are revised figures throughout and a genuine real-time forecaster
would have had worse inputs than these regressions were given. This affects all
four forecasts in the same direction and is unlikely to drive the comparison
between them, but it inflates the level of accuracy reported.

Finally, we test four horizons in each of eleven regions, and report both
per-region counts and a panel statistic per horizon. We do not implement a
uniform multi-horizon test in the sense of \citet{quaedvlieg2021}, which would
control size across the horizon set jointly. Given that the panel statistics
range from $4.01$ to $-1.03$ and change sign between horizons, we do not expect
such a test to alter the conclusion, but it would sharpen the statement about
the four-quarter cell where our two criteria disagree.

\section{Conclusion}

A financial conditions index for a euro-area country carries information about
unemployment one and two quarters ahead that survives comparison with a random
walk in ten and nine of eleven regions, with panel Clark--West statistics of
$4.0$ and $3.2$. At two years it survives nothing: the index loses to a random
walk in every region and the panel statistic is $-1.0$, while the same
forecasts appear to win in ten of eleven regions when scored against a
recursively estimated autoregression.

The mechanism is not specific to financial conditions indices, to this index or
to this panel. It applies whenever a persistent target is forecast from a short
sample and the benchmark estimates a coefficient that could have been imposed.
Our simulations show that a predictor with genuine content at every horizon
still registers as valuable against such a benchmark at persistences where a
forecaster would be better served by a random walk, and that the Clark--West
test correctly reports the predictor's presence in the regression throughout.

The practical implication is a reporting standard rather than a new method.
Evaluations at horizons of a year or more should include a benchmark that
imposes the persistence of the target, and should state which benchmark the
reported ratios are against. On this evidence the difference between those two
choices is larger than the difference between having an index and not having
one.
